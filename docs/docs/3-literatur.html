2  Theoretical Background

This chapter sets out the concepts the thesis relies on: landscape connectivity and its modeling, the movement ecology of the wild boar, and the epidemiology of African Swine Fever (ASF) that together make connectivity a disease-management variable.

2.1 Ecological Connectivity in Fragmented Landscapes

Landscape connectivity describes the degree to which a landscape facilitates or impedes the movement of organisms among habitat patches. Taylor et al. (1993) formalised the concept as a vital structural element of landscapes, and it is now standard to separate two dimensions. Structural connectivity refers to the physical arrangement of habitat in space and can be read from land cover maps alone. Functional connectivity reflects the behavioural response of a specific species to that structure (Urbina et al. 2023; Tischendorf et al. 2003), so two structurally identical landscapes can differ greatly depending on whether the target species treats roads, rivers, or fields as permeable or as barriers. A resistance surface calibrated for one species therefore cannot simply be reused for another. This distinction matters in the Swiss Mittelland, which looks connected on a coarse map but is functionally fragmented for large mammals by dense road and rail networks, urban development, and intensive agriculture, leading to reduced gene flow and limited recolonisation for species such as red deer and wild boar (Fischer et al. 2024; Urbina et al. 2023).

2.2 Modeling Functional Connectivity

2.2.1 Resistance Surfaces and the Integrated Step-Selection Function

The resistance surface is the core spatial input to all functional connectivity models. It is a raster in which each cell carries a value representing the relative cost of movement, with high-quality habitat assigned low resistance and barriers such as motorways assigned very high or infinite resistance. Resistance can be assigned from expert judgement or, more defensibly, estimated empirically from GPS tracking. Urbina et al. (2023) derived resistance for red deer from over 85,000 GPS fixes of 15 individuals across the Greater Geneva agglomeration at 5-metre resolution, and Fischer et al. (2024) applied a comparable empirical framework to the Swiss Mittelland. Finer resolution resolves the narrow infrastructure that constrains large-mammal movement but multiplies the cell count and the computational cost.

Where GPS data are available, the integrated step-selection function (iSSF) provides a principled basis for this estimation (Avgar et al. 2016). The iSSF reconstructs movement as a sequence of steps, each with a step length and turning angle, and compares every observed step against random alternatives drawn from the same movement kernel. Fitting a conditional logistic regression of the form \(w(x) = \exp\!\left(\sum_k \hat{\beta}_k x_k\right)\) yields selection coefficients \(\hat{\beta}_k\) that express habitat preference while holding movement capacity and availability constant. This separates genuine preference from mere abundance: an animal seen often in forest may prefer forest, or forest may simply be plentiful where it started, and the conditional design distinguishes the two. To turn the coefficients into resistance on the scale required by corridor models, Keeley et al. (2016) proposed the transformation \(R = \exp\!\left(-c \cdot \hat{\beta}_k x_k\right)\), where the sign is reversed so that strongly selected cells receive low resistance. A single tracking dataset therefore yields a resistance surface that is both species-specific and statistically grounded.

2.2.2 Least-Cost Paths and Circuit Theory

Least-cost path (LCP) analysis was among the first methods to use resistance surfaces for corridor mapping (Adriaensen et al. 2003). It identifies the single route between two patches that minimises cumulative resistance. It is computationally cheap and widely used, but it assumes movement along one optimal route and says nothing about whether alternative routes exist or how redundant the surrounding landscape is (McRae et al. 2008).

Circuit theory addresses that limitation by treating the resistance surface as an electrical network in which current flows from a source to a ground through all available pathways at once (McRae et al. 2008). McRae et al. (2008) showed that the current through any cell is proportional to the expected number of times a random walker would pass through it, giving the method a process-based interpretation that LCP lacks. High current density marks heavily used corridors, and points where the landscape forces nearly all movement through a single passage are called pinch points, which are the highest-priority targets for intervention because losing a small area there disproportionately reduces connectivity. Because effective resistance falls as parallel routes are added, the method also captures redundancy, and the ratio of least-cost to resistance distance measures how much a single bottleneck dominates. The Circuitscape software solves networks of millions of cells, making it applicable at landscape scale. Urbina et al. (2023) applied Circuitscape 5 to a graph of 172 core areas and 424 links in Greater Geneva, mapping the major corridors and confirming the role of specific wildlife overpasses.

2.2.3 Infection-Risk Kernels and Cost-Distance Dispersal

An infection-risk kernel converts a cost-distance analysis into the probability that a dispersing infected animal reaches a given location before dying. Dispersal is described by an exponential kernel \(P(d) = \exp(-d/D)\), where \(d\) is a distance measure and \(D\) is a characteristic dispersal scale set from telemetry. The single parameter \(D\) has a direct probabilistic interpretation and can be read from the literature. For wild boar, the relevant scale during the infectious period is the mean natal dispersal distance rather than the home-range diameter, because infected animals move normally until the final clinical phase. Telemetry and mark-recapture studies across European populations report mean dispersal of approximately 4 kilometres, with most events within 10 kilometres of the birth site (Truvé and Lemel 2003; Keuling et al. 2010; Prévot and Licoppe 2013; Morelle et al. 2015). Critically, \(d\) is taken as Dijkstra cost-distance through the resistance surface rather than straight-line distance, so the kernel assigns near-zero risk to cells that are geographically close but separated by a motorway or large water body. The EFSA operational framework gives a validation benchmark (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) 2018): the 3-kilometre protection zone corresponds to a risk of about 0.50 under the kernel with \(D = 4\) km, and the 10-kilometre surveillance boundary to about 0.08, which lets the displayed risk classes be anchored to recognised thresholds.

2.2.4 Biased Correlated Random Walk

A biased correlated random walk (BCRW) models movement as discrete steps shaped by two forces: directional memory, which correlates successive steps, and an environmental bias toward low-cost terrain (Turchin 1998; Codling et al. 2008). Directional persistence is described by the von Mises distribution, where the probability of a turn \(\theta\) is proportional to \(\exp(\kappa \cos\theta)\) and the concentration \(\kappa\) controls how strongly the walker continues in its current direction. Weighting each candidate step by the inverse of destination resistance and step length adds the cost bias. As the number of walks grows, the BCRW visit density converges toward the circuit-theory current map under the Randomized Shortest Paths equivalence (Saerens et al. 2009), so the two methods estimate the same quantity by different means. In practice this limit is reached only with very many walks, which makes the BCRW a useful complementary diagnostic rather than a replacement for circuit theory.

2.3 Wild Boar Ecology Relevant to Connectivity

The wild boar (Sus scrofa) is a native Eurasian ungulate whose European populations have expanded strongly in recent decades, driven by milder winters, the spread of maize and other high-energy crops, reduced hunting pressure, and supplementary winter feeding (Guberti et al. 2019). Wild boar are habitat generalists that use woodland, especially deciduous and mixed forest with mast resources, as daytime resting cover and forage at night in agricultural fields, showing marked behavioural plasticity in fragmented and peri-urban landscapes (Morelle et al. 2015). This forest dependence for cover and avoidance of human infrastructure is what a resistance surface must capture. Home ranges are usually well below 50 square kilometres and movement contracts where food is stable and abundant (Guberti et al. 2019; Morelle et al. 2015). The epidemiologically important exception is natal dispersal by young males, which can cover much greater distances and, rarely, exceed 100 kilometres (Guberti et al. 2019), because a dispersing animal in the incubation phase can carry infection into previously unaffected areas.

2.4 African Swine Fever and the Connectivity Trade-off

ASF is caused by a large DNA virus of the family Asfarviridae that in Europe affects only domestic pigs and wild boar (Guberti et al. 2019). The genotype II strain of the current epidemic has a case fatality rate of 90 to 95 percent, most infected animals die within four to nine days of showing signs, and no licensed vaccine exists, so control depends on surveillance, movement restriction, and population management. The virus is exceptionally stable, remaining infectious in blood for up to 15 weeks and persisting in carcasses for months, so infected carcasses act as long-lived reservoirs that decouple persistence from host density (Guberti et al. 2019). Transmission runs through direct contact, which depends on host density, and through contact with carcasses and fomites, which sustains the cycle even as the live population declines. Since its introduction to the Caucasus in 2007, ASF has advanced westward at roughly 1 to 3 kilometres per month through wild boar populations, with long-distance human-mediated incursions seeding new clusters ahead of the front, leaving currently disease-free countries including Switzerland at near-term risk (Guberti et al. 2019).

The more connected and continuous the wild boar population, the faster the epidemic front advances, while natural or artificial barriers can slow it. This makes connectivity a genuine management variable: authorities can in principle reduce spread by reinforcing barriers, installing targeted fencing in high-connectivity areas, or lowering density in specific corridors. Doing so effectively requires knowing where the corridors and pinch points are and predicting how an intervention would change them. Hess (1994) placed this on a formal footing, showing that conservation corridors generally aid metapopulation persistence but, under conditions of high direct-contact transmission and high disease-induced mortality that closely match ASF, can substantially raise the risk of disease-driven extinction. The same corridors that benefit red deer can therefore accelerate ASF in wild boar. Existing models, including those of Fischer et al. (2024) and Urbina et al. (2023), are rigorous but static and cannot answer what-if questions about a proposed fence or overpass. Building a tool that combines their methodological rigour with interactive, user-defined scenarios for wild boar in the Canton of Zürich is the central task of this thesis.
