3  Material and Methods

The methodology has two phases. The first builds an empirically calibrated wild boar resistance surface from GPS telemetry. The second translates that surface into an interactive QGIS plugin. Full dataset information is given in Appendix A, and the complete parameter settings of the plugin modules are listed in the statistical appendix.

3.1 Software Environment and Data Inputs

The ecological algorithm was developed in R 4.5.1, using terra and sf for spatial handling, amt for movement metrics and step generation, the tidyverse packages for data wrangling, survival for the conditional logistic regression, and car for collinearity diagnostics. The plugin was implemented in Python for QGIS 3.40.13 as Wildboar Connectivity (ASF), version 0.6. It builds on the QGIS API (qgis.core, qgis.gui, qgis.PyQt) for the interface, map tools and layer handling, numpy and rasterio for raster reading, writing and georeferencing, and scipy for the sparse-matrix circuit-theory solver and connected-component analysis that underlie the pinchpoint and least-cost-path modules. Circuit-theory pinchpoint analysis is primarily delegated to the official Julia package Circuitscape.jl, invoked as an external subprocess, with the scipy-based solver serving as a fallback when Julia is not available on the user’s system. Exact package versions are listed in the reproducibility appendix.

Canton Aargau served as the training area where the resistance model was calibrated, and Canton Zürich as the independent transfer area where the plugin was evaluated. A master raster grid was generated for each canton in the Swiss LV95 reference system (EPSG:2056) at 25-metre resolution. To avoid edge effects, each cantonal boundary was buffered by 1,000 metres and converted to a rectangular processing extent, which was then intersected with the national boundary because the high-resolution covariates are restricted to Swiss territory. Extending the study area into Germany was rejected because harmonising Swiss and German topographic and land cover data would have required reconciling incompatible classifications and resolutions, and pan-European products such as CORINE are too coarse to resolve the narrow forest corridors and minor roads that govern wild boar movement at a 15-minute fix interval.

The biological foundation was a GPS telemetry dataset of 16 wild boar tracked in the Aargau study area between 2014 and 2017 at a 15-minute interval, collected by the Wildlife Management Research Group (WILMA, ZHAW) in the Wildschweinschreck project. This dataset enabled a strictly empirical resistance surface rather than one based on expert opinion. Environmental covariates were derived from swissTLM3D (2026 edition) for forest, roads, railways, settlements, and water, and from the DHM25 elevation model for terrain. Administrative extents came from swissBOUNDARIES3D. Three further datasets supported the transfer to Zürich. The national wildlife passages geodatabase from the FOEN was used to restore permeability at verified crossings. The permanent ASP fence shapefile from the WILMA research group was used as an absolute barrier. The WILMA potential core-habitat layer for Zürich was used as least-cost-path targets. The role, version, and coordinate system of each dataset are tabulated in the data sources appendix Appendix A.

3.2 Resistance Surface Pipeline

The pipeline runs in three stages: preprocessing and behavioural classification of the telemetry data, empirical estimation of habitat preference with an iSSF, and transformation of the coefficients into resistance with the Keeley et al. (2016) exponential function. The design follows the connectivity frameworks of Fischer et al. (2024), Clontz et al. (2021), and Keeley et al. (2016).

3.2.1 Telemetry Processing and Step Classification

All geometries were standardised to LV95. Records with missing or implausible coordinates were removed, timestamps were standardised to UTC, and the first five days of each deployment were dropped as a capture-shock buffer. Deployments with fewer than 100 fixes or spanning under 30 days were excluded, as were the fixes with a biologically unrealistic step length (> 3000m) (Podgórski et al. 2013). Trajectories were resampled to a 15-minute interval with a five-minute tolerance and segmented into temporally continuous bursts, so that step lengths and turning angles were never computed across a data gap.

Because a resistance surface models the cost of travelling between habitat patches rather than behaviour within them, the steps were separated into in-patch (stationary, resting, foraging) and in-matrix (movement, directed transit, migration) states. The fractal and mixture-model approach used for red deer by Fischer et al. (2024) proved unsuitable, because wild boar are highly resident and their transit and resting modes are far less spatially separated than in migratory red deer. A rule-based classifier was therefore developed, applied within each burst, which labels a step as in-matrix if it meets any of three criteria. The first is a step length above 250 metres (Podgórski et al. 2013). The second is a step length above 150 metres combined with a turning angle below \(\pi/4\) in absolute value (Clontz et al. 2021). The third is three or more consecutive steps each above 100 metres with a turning angle below \(\pi/4\) (Benhamou 2014). All other steps were classified as in-patch, and only the in-matrix steps were retained for fitting.

3.2.2 Spatial Covariates

Covariates were derived on the 25-metre master grid using the buffered extent so that focal statistics near the boundary were unbiased.

A binary forest mask was created by extracting relevant forest classes (“Wald”, “Wald offen”, “Gebueschwald”, and “Gehoelzflaeche”) from the swissTLM3D layer ‘tlm_bb_bodenbedeckung’. During the rasterisation, the all-touched parameter ensured to preserve narrow tree lines. Then a 50-metre and a 1000-metre focal window was used to capture forest density. These two layers reflected immediate cover and broader landscape context, representing two different spatial orders of habitat selection (Johnson 1980). A distance-to-forest-edge layer was also defined to analyse the behaviour of wild boar in forests. This layer was created by calculating the euclidean distance from a pixel outside of the forest to the nearest forest pixel and inside the forest to the nearest non-forest pixel.

Topographic variables (slope and the Terrain Ruggedness Index) were derived from the DHM25 digital elevation model using the terrain function. Because the raw ASCII file lacked an embedded Coordinate Reference System (CRS), the LV03 projection was explicitly assigned before cropping the data to the study area. After computing the terrain metrics, the resulting continuous layers were reprojected onto the LV95 master grid using bilinear interpolation.

Spatial data for standing and flowing water bodies were extracted from two highly detailed swissTLM3D layers (tlm_gewaesser_stehendes_gewaesser and tlm_gewaesser_fliessgewaesser). Because standing water features are natively structured as shoreline line strings, a custom geometric operation was applied to merge these segments and cast them into solid polygons. Known island geometries were explicitly subtracted from these features to accurately represent pure water surfaces. Flowing water features were filtered to retain only above-ground segments (e.g., surface flow, bridges, waterfalls), excluding subterranean conduits. Following rasterization, Euclidean distance layers were computed across the study extent for three distinct categories: all water bodies combined, standing water exclusively, and flowing water exclusively. A distinct binary mask was also generated for large standing water bodies exceeding a 1km² threshold, allowing them to be included as absolute barriers in the resistance surface.

Spatial data representing human infrastructure were extracted from different swissTLM3D layers (tlm_strassen_strasse, tlm_oev_eisenbahn and tlm_namen_siedlungsname). To ensure only features acting as physical barriers or behavioral deterrents to wild boar were retained, the data underwent rigorous preprocessing. All three-dimensional geometries were flattened to 2D, and subterranean features (e.g., road and railway tunnels) were explicitly removed based on their elevation level and infrastructure type codes. Railway networks were also filtered to exclude disused tracks, funiculars, cog railways, and museum lines, as these do not constitute meaningful movement barriers. Road networks were hierarchically classified into fenced roads (highways), main roads, and secondary roads (e.g., local and forest tracks). Settlements were extracted as contiguous built-up polygons rather than individual building footprints. To model the effect of human proximity on habitat selection, continuous Euclidean distance rasters were generated for fenced roads, main roads, secondary roads, railways, and settlements. For subsequent connectivity modeling, infrastructure layers were categorized by their assumed permeability. Secondary roads were maintained solely as graded proximity covariates, as wild boar routinely cross narrow forest tracks. Conversely, a combined soft-barrier mask was generated comprising main roads, railways, and settlements. Fenced highways were isolated into a discrete binary mask to be parameterized as absolute movement barriers. Finally, known wildlife passages were imported, buffered by 30 meters to represent functional crossing zones, and rasterized to serve as localized permeability features capable of overriding absolute highway barriers.

3.2.3 Integrated Step-Selection Function

The Integrated Step Selection Function was fitted exclusively on the in matrix steps. Each observed step was compared against ten random control steps drawn from the empirical gamma step length and von Mises turning angle distributions, consistent with established practice (Avgar et al. 2016). Prior to model formulation, steps lacking angular measurements or exhibiting a length of exactly zero were excluded. Two movement kernel terms were included to ensure habitat selection was estimated independently of the underlying movement process: the cosine of the turning angle and the natural logarithm of the step length plus one. Covariate values were extracted at each step endpoint. All continuous covariates were standardized using the training mean and standard deviation from the Aargau data. These scaling parameters were saved to ensure the identical transformation could be applied to the Zurich projection.

A variable selection step removed seven of the fourteen candidate covariates. The seven retained ones, all demonstrated variance inflation factors below 1.5 and were therefore included in the final model. This final model was fitted using conditional logistic regression stratified by step ID, applying the Efron approximation for tied event times.

3.2.4 Resistance Surface Construction and Transfer

The conversion from selection coefficients to spatial resistance followed the three-stage framework outlined by Keeley et al. (2016). First, the linear predictor was calculated for every cell as the weighted sum of the scaled covariates:

\[\eta = \sum_k \hat{\beta}_k \cdot x_{k,\text{scaled}},\]

This predictor was normalized to a habitat suitability surface \(S \in [0,1]\) using local min-max scaling. Finally, suitability was transformed into a resistance surface using the negative exponential function:

\[R = \exp\!\left(C \cdot (1 - S)\right), \qquad C = 4,\]

A scaling constant of \(C = 4\) was applied (Fischer et al. 2024), yielding a theoretical resistance range from 1 for the optimal habitat to approximately 54.6 for the least preferred.

Following the continuous transformation, a deterministic burn-in assigned absolute values to discrete landscape features in a strict hierarchical order. Infrastructure cells comprising main roads, active railways, and settlements received the maximum continuous resistance value (\(R_{max}\)). Fenced motorways and large lakes (exceeding \(\text{1km}^2\)) were assigned as NoData to act as absolute, impassable barriers. Finally, verified wildlife passages were assigned the minimum resistance (\(R = 1\)), ensuring these specific mitigation structures remained fully permeable and actively overrode underlying highway barriers.

To project this model, the identical covariates were computed for the Canton Zürich extent. The linear predictor was calculated using the Aargau selection coefficients and scaling parameters without re-estimation. Importantly, the final normalization utilized the Zürich-specific linear predictor range rather than the Aargau baseline. This localized scaling ensures the full \([0,1]\) suitability spectrum is utilized, maximizing spatial contrast for within-Zürich corridor identification. While this approach renders absolute resistance values non-comparable across cantons, it is optimal for intra-cantonal connectivity planning. The burn-in hierarchy was applied identically to the Zürich surface, with the addition of permanent African Swine Fever (ASF) prevention fences, which were parameterized as absolute NoData barriers immediately prior to the wildlife passage burn-in.

3.3 QGIS Plugin Development

The second phase translated the validated resistance surface into an interactive decision-support tool. The Wildboar Connectivity (ASF) plugin lets a wildlife or veterinary user load the pre-computed resistance raster, define a hypothetical outbreak location with a map click, draw virtual fences or place wildlife overpasses on the canvas, and immediately recompute connectivity to compare scenarios. All computation runs in a background thread so the interface stays responsive.

3.3.1 Architecture and Interaction Model

The plugin is organised as cooperating Python modules with bounded responsibilities: an entry-point module that registers the plugin and owns the dialog and map tools, two custom map tools for point selection and polyline drawing, a stateless resistance-editor that returns a modified copy of the array without ever writing to the source raster, a background task that implements the five analyses, and a subprocess wrapper for the Circuitscape.jl solver. Communication between user actions and logic uses the Qt signal-and-slot mechanism, so the drawing tools carry out no computation themselves. The dialog exposes a resistance-raster selector, an optional habitat-polygon selector for least-cost-path targets, the three drawing tools, and five checkboxes selecting which analyses to run.

3.3.2 Landscape Modification

When the user runs an analysis, accumulated fences and overpasses are transformed into the raster coordinate system and applied to a deep copy of the loaded resistance window, so the source file is never altered. Fence polylines are rasterised with all-touched coverage and dilated to a two-cell-wide ribbon of NaN, making them indistinguishable from the absolute barriers already encoded in the surface. Overpasses are stamped as small discs of minimum resistance, and this overwrite is bidirectional, so an overpass placed on a motorway replaces the NaN with a passable value, correctly representing a crossing punched through an otherwise impassable structure.

3.3.3 Analysis Window, Source, and Cost Grid

The analysis is restricted to a window centred on the outbreak origin, extending 10,000 metres plus a 1,500-metre edge buffer in each direction and snapped to the integer pixel grid of the source so that outputs are not shifted relative to the input. The outbreak source is a disc of finite-resistance cells around the origin, snapped to the nearest passable cell if the click lands on a barrier. From the source disc, a single Dijkstra sweep computes the cost grid. The sweep uses an 8-connected metric that also penalises diagonal moves, so each reachable cell receives the lowest total cost of getting there from the origin. Cells behind absolute barriers stay at infinite cost. These unreachable cells mark the edge of the area of interest, the part of the landscape the outbreak can actually reach.

3.3.4 The Five Analyses

The least-cost path module connects the origin to the nearest reachable habitat clusters and traces each route back through the Dijkstra grid. These clusters are either taken from a user-supplied habitat layer or auto-detected by thresholding the resistance raster. Shared segments accumulate strength so that the backbone corridor serving many or large clusters is highlighted over the smaller corridors.

The infection-risk module converts the cost grid into a continuous probability surface, \(\text{risk}(d) = \exp(-d/D_{\text{cost}})\), where \(D_{\text{cost}}\) rescales the published 4-kilometre mean wild boar dispersal distance into cost units. This is done by using the pixel size and the median resistance of the window. The results are divided into three zones based on EFSA guidelines, with the high-risk category corresponding to the 3-kilometer protection zone and the medium-risk category corresponding to the 10-kilometer surveillance zone (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) 2018).

The pinchpoint module treats the window as a resistor network and injects current from the source to the boundary, identifying where movement concentrates. The preferred solver is Circuitscape.jl, which is called as a Julia subprocess. A scipy Laplacian solver written entirely in Python is used as a fallback. Although it is slower, it produces equivalent results.

The biased correlated random walk releases many walkers from the source disc, each selecting steps by the product of a cost weight and a von Mises directional-persistence weight. Then it accumulates a visit-density surface that provides an independent stochastic check on the pinchpoint result (Panzacchi et al. 2016).

The iSSF-based individual movement model simulates infected animals from the origin. Each is running for a stochastically drawn infectious period and selecting steps by habitat suitability. In this case the habitat suitability is recovered by inverting the Keeley transformation, \(S = 1 - \ln R / C\) on the resistance surface. The fraction of animals visiting each cell gives a contamination-probability surface that quantifies disease reach rather than movement capacity alone. The infectious-period distribution and the movement-kernel parameters of these modules are calibrated to EFSA virological data and to ASF studies in European wild boar (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) 2018; Probst et al. 2017; Chenais et al. 2019).
