3  Material and Methods

The methodology has two phases. The first builds an empirically calibrated wild boar resistance surface from GPS telemetry. The second translates that surface into an interactive QGIS plugin. Full dataset provenance is given in Appendix D and the data sources appendix, and the complete parameter settings of the plugin modules are listed in the statistical appendix, so this chapter keeps to the methodological decisions and their rationale.

3.1 Software Environment and Data Inputs

The ecological algorithm was developed in R 4.5.1, using terra and sf for spatial handling, amt for movement metrics and step generation, the tidyverse packages for data wrangling, survival for the conditional logistic regression, and car for collinearity diagnostics. The plugin was implemented in Python for QGIS 3.40.13 as Wildboar Connectivity (ASF), version 0.4. Exact package versions are listed in the reproducibility appendix.

Canton Aargau served as the training area where the resistance model was calibrated, and Canton Zürich as the independent transfer area where the plugin was evaluated. A master raster grid was generated for each canton in the Swiss LV95 reference system (EPSG:2056) at 25-metre resolution. To avoid edge effects, each cantonal boundary was buffered by 2,000 metres and converted to a rectangular processing extent, which was then intersected with the national boundary because the high-resolution covariates are restricted to Swiss territory. Extending the study area into Germany was rejected because harmonising Swiss and German topographic and land cover data would have required reconciling incompatible classifications and resolutions, and pan-European products such as CORINE are too coarse to resolve the narrow forest corridors and minor roads that govern wild boar movement at a 15-minute fix interval.

The biological foundation was a GPS telemetry dataset of 36 wild boar tracked in the Aargau study areas of Elfingen and Felsenau between 2014 and 2017 at a 15-minute interval, collected by the Wildlife Management Research Group (WILMA, ZHAW) in the Wildschweinschreck project. This dataset enabled a strictly empirical resistance surface rather than one based on expert opinion. Environmental covariates were derived from swissTLM3D (2026 edition) for forest, roads, railways, settlements, and water, and from the DHM25 elevation model for terrain. Administrative extents came from swissBOUNDARIES3D. Three further datasets supported the transfer to Zürich. The national wildlife passages geodatabase from the FOEN was used to restore permeability at verified crossings. The permanent ASP fence shapefile from the Cantonal Veterinary Office of Zürich was used as an absolute barrier. The WILMA potential core-habitat layer for Zürich was used as a spatial validation anchor and as least-cost-path targets. The role, version, and coordinate system of each dataset are tabulated in the data sources appendix.

3.2 Resistance Surface Pipeline

The pipeline runs in three stages: regularisation and behavioural classification of the telemetry, empirical estimation of habitat preference with an iSSF, and transformation of the coefficients into resistance with the Keeley et al. (2016) exponential function. The design follows the connectivity frameworks of Fischer et al. (2024), Clontz et al. (2021), and Keeley et al. (2016).

3.2.1 Telemetry Processing and Step Classification

All geometries were standardised to LV95. Records with missing or implausible coordinates were removed, timestamps were standardised to UTC, and the first five days of each deployment were dropped as a capture-shock buffer. Deployments with fewer than 100 fixes or spanning under 30 days were excluded, as was one short re-collaring deployment with poor fix quality (the exclusion criteria and the affected deployment are documented in the data sources appendix). Trajectories were resampled to a 15-minute interval with a five-minute tolerance and segmented into temporally continuous bursts, so that step lengths and turning angles were never computed across a data gap. The cleaned dataset yielded 132,388 classified steps.

Because a resistance surface models the cost of travelling between habitat patches rather than behaviour within them, the steps were separated into in-patch and in-matrix (directed transit) states. The fractal and mixture-model approach used for red deer by Fischer et al. (2024) proved unsuitable, because wild boar are highly resident and their transit and resting modes are far less spatially separated than in migratory red deer. A rule-based classifier was therefore developed, applied within each burst, which labels a step as in-matrix if it meets any of three criteria. The first is a step length above 250 metres (Podgórski et al. 2013). The second is a step length above 150 metres combined with a turning angle below \(\pi/4\) in absolute value (Clontz et al. 2021). The third is three or more consecutive steps each above 100 metres with a turning angle below \(\pi/4\) (Benhamou 2014). All other steps were classified as in-patch, and only the in-matrix steps were retained for fitting.

3.2.2 Spatial Covariates

Covariates were derived on the 25-metre master grid using the buffered extent so that focal statistics near the boundary were unbiased. Forest was extracted from swissTLM3D as a binary mask (all-touched rasterisation to preserve narrow tree lines), together with forest density in a 50-metre and a 1000-metre focal window to capture immediate cover and broader landscape context, representing two spatial orders of habitat selection (Johnson 1980), and an unsigned distance-to-forest-edge layer. Slope and the Terrain Ruggedness Index were derived from the DHM25. A combined distance-to-water layer was computed as the cell-wise minimum of distance to flowing water and to standing water, with lakes above one square kilometre held back as absolute barriers. Roads were classified into fenced high-performance roads, main roads, and secondary roads, and combined with railways and settlement polygons. Subsurface segments such as tunnels and underpasses were removed before rasterisation so they do not act as false barriers, and out-of-service and specialty rail lines were dropped. Euclidean distance rasters were computed for each infrastructure class. The exact object-type filters, attribute values, and processing functions are documented in the data sources appendix. Distance to fenced roads was computed but deliberately excluded from the model to avoid a barrier-trapping artifact, described in the variable selection step below.

3.2.3 Integrated Step-Selection Function

The iSSF was fitted on in-matrix steps only, each compared against ten random control steps drawn from the empirical gamma step-length and von Mises turning-angle distributions, which is consistent with established practice (Avgar et al. 2016). Two movement-kernel terms, the log step length and the cosine of the turning angle, were included so that habitat selection is estimated independently of the movement process itself. Covariate values were extracted at each step endpoint, and all continuous covariates were z-standardised using the Aargau training mean and standard deviation, saved as a parameter vector so the same scaling can be reapplied unchanged to Zürich.

A variable selection step removed seven of the fourteen candidate covariates. Slope and the Terrain Ruggedness Index showed extreme mutual collinearity (variance inflation factor above 80) and were non-significant, so both were dropped. Binary forest cover and distance to forest edge were redundant once the two density layers were present. The separate standing and flowing water distances were merged into the combined layer. Distance to fenced roads was excluded because impassable fences force animals to pace along them, producing dense telemetry clusters that a step-selection model misreads as attraction, an artifact addressed instead through the deterministic burn-in. The final model used the clogit function stratified by step, with the seven retained covariates, all of which had variance inflation factors below 1.4.

3.2.4 Resistance Surface Construction and Transfer

The conversion from coefficients to resistance followed the three stages of Keeley et al. (2016). The linear predictor was formed for every cell as the weighted sum of the scaled covariates,

\[\eta = \sum_k \hat{\beta}_k \cdot x_{k,\text{scaled}},\]

normalised to a suitability surface \(S \in [0,1]\) by min-max scaling, and transformed to resistance by

\[R = \exp\!\left(C \cdot (1 - S)\right), \qquad C = 4,\]

as used by Fischer et al. (2024), giving a theoretical range of 1 for the most preferred habitat to about 54.6 for the least. A deterministic burn-in then assigned absolute values in a fixed order, because later assignments overwrite earlier ones where masks overlap: small water bodies received a high finite cost, all infrastructure cells received the maximum resistance, fenced motorways and large lakes were set to NoData as absolute barriers, and finally wildlife passages were set to the minimum resistance so that verified crossings remain fully permeable.

The Zürich surface was built by recomputing identical covariates for the Zürich extent and applying the Aargau coefficients and scaling parameters without re-estimation. The only change was that the final normalisation used the Zürich linear-predictor range rather than the Aargau range, so that the full suitability scale is used for within-Zürich differentiation. This choice maximises corridor contrast within Zürich at the cost of making absolute resistance values non-comparable between cantons, which is acceptable because the analysis is confined to Zürich. The burn-in was applied identically, with the permanent ASP fences added as absolute barriers before the wildlife-passage step.

3.3 QGIS Plugin Development

The second phase translated the validated resistance surface into an interactive decision-support tool. The Wildboar Connectivity (ASF) plugin lets a wildlife or veterinary user load the pre-computed resistance raster, define a hypothetical outbreak location with a map click, draw virtual fences or place wildlife overpasses on the canvas, and immediately recompute connectivity to compare scenarios. All computation runs in a background thread so the interface stays responsive.

3.3.1 Architecture and Interaction Model

The plugin is organised as cooperating Python modules with bounded responsibilities: an entry-point module that registers the plugin and owns the dialog and map tools, two custom map tools for point selection and polyline drawing, a stateless resistance-editor that returns a modified copy of the array without ever writing to the source raster, a background task that implements the five analyses, and a subprocess wrapper for the Circuitscape.jl solver. Communication between user actions and logic uses the Qt signal-and-slot mechanism, so the drawing tools carry out no computation themselves. The dialog exposes a resistance-raster selector, an optional habitat-polygon selector for least-cost-path targets, the three drawing tools, and five checkboxes selecting which analyses to run.

3.3.2 Landscape Modification

When the user runs an analysis, accumulated fences and overpasses are transformed into the raster coordinate system and applied to a deep copy of the loaded resistance window, so the source file is never altered. Fence polylines are rasterised with all-touched coverage and dilated to a two-cell-wide ribbon of NaN, making them indistinguishable from the absolute barriers already encoded in the surface. Overpasses are stamped as small discs of minimum resistance, and this overwrite is bidirectional, so an overpass placed on a motorway replaces the NaN with a passable value, correctly representing a crossing punched through an otherwise impassable structure.

3.3.3 Analysis Window, Source, and Cost Grid

The analysis is restricted to a window centred on the outbreak origin, extending 10,000 metres plus a 1,500-metre edge buffer in each direction and snapped to the integer pixel grid of the source so that outputs are not shifted relative to the input. The outbreak source is a disc of finite-resistance cells around the origin, snapped to the nearest passable cell if the click lands on a barrier. A single Dijkstra sweep from the source disc, using an 8-connected geometric cost metric that penalises diagonal moves, produces a cost grid giving every reachable cell its minimum cumulative cost, while cells behind absolute barriers receive infinite cost and define the boundary of the reachable area of interest.

3.3.4 The Five Analyses

The least-cost path module connects the origin to the nearest reachable habitat clusters, taken either from a user-supplied habitat layer or auto-detected by thresholding the resistance raster, and traces each route back through the Dijkstra grid. Shared segments accumulate strength so that the backbone corridor serving many or large clusters is highlighted, and the network is styled in graduated blue.

The infection-risk module converts the cost grid into a continuous probability surface, \(\text{risk}(d) = \exp(-d/D_{\text{cost}})\), where \(D_{\text{cost}}\) rescales the published 4-kilometre mean wild boar dispersal distance into cost units using the pixel size and the median resistance of the window. The result is classified into three EFSA-aligned zones, with the high-risk class anchored to the 3-kilometre protection zone and the medium class to the 10-kilometre surveillance zone (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) 2018).

The pinchpoint module treats the window as a resistor network and injects current from the source to the boundary, identifying where movement concentrates. The preferred solver is Circuitscape.jl, called as a Julia subprocess, with a pure-Python scipy sparse Laplacian solver as a fallback that produces equivalent results more slowly.

The biased correlated random walk releases many walkers from the source disc, each selecting steps by the product of a cost weight and a von Mises directional-persistence weight, and accumulates a visit-density surface that provides an independent stochastic check on the pinchpoint result, toward which it converges at large walk numbers (Saerens et al. 2009).

The iSSF-based individual movement model simulates infected animals from the origin, each running for a stochastically drawn infectious period and selecting steps by habitat suitability recovered by inverting the Keeley transformation, \(S = 1 - \ln R / C\). The fraction of animals visiting each cell gives a contamination-probability surface that quantifies disease reach rather than movement capacity alone. The infectious-period distribution and the movement-kernel parameters of these modules are calibrated to EFSA virological data and to ASF studies in European wild boar (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) 2018; Probst et al. 2017; Chenais et al. 2019), and the full numerical settings are listed in the statistical appendix.
