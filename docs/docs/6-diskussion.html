5  Discussion

This thesis set out to adapt a connectivity modeling framework for wild boar by building an empirically calibrated resistance surface, to develop an interactive QGIS plugin for testing landscape modifications, and to produce scenario-contrast visualisations for wildlife authorities in Canton Zürich. The following sections conclude and evaluate how far each objective was reached, place the findings in context, and set out the principal uncertainties.

5.1 Ecological Interpretation of the iSSF Model

All seven retained covariates were positive predictors of transit movement, confirming that wild boar in directed movement prefer denser forest at both scales and greater distance from every class of transport and settlement infrastructure. This matches the established ecology of the species, which depends on continuous forest cover for movement and strongly avoids traffic, light, and disturbance (Morelle et al. 2015; Guberti et al. 2019). Two features of the coefficient structure stand out. Forest density at the 50-metre scale carried by far the smallest standard error, reflecting the central role of immediate cover. Distance to railway carried a nearly identical point estimate but with a much larger standard error, most likely because railways are sparse in the Aargau training area and few transit steps were recorded near them, so the avoidance effect is real but its magnitude is less certain.

The positive coefficients at both forest scales indicate selection for cells that are locally wooded and embedded in a forested surrounding, consistent with hierarchical habitat selection (Avgar et al. 2016). The excluded binary forest cover layer essentially acts as a coarser version of the 50-metre forest density layer and therefore adds no further information to the model. Distance to forest edge, on the other hand, does not appear to capture a pattern that is relevant to in-matrix movement. During directed transit, wild boar are not actively seeking out forest edges. Instead, they favour dense forest cover that offers continuous concealment along the route. The tendency to stay close to forest edges is more characteristic of in-patch behaviour, where animals benefit from quick access to foraging in adjacent agricultural fields while remaining within reach of cover. In addition, transit routes regularly pass through both forested and open sections, so the distance to forest edge varies in both directions along a single path and the variable ends up carrying no consistent signal.

The non-significance of slope and terrain ruggedness can be explained by the relief of the training landscape itself rather than by a genuine lack of influence on wild boar movement. Canton Aargau and also the extrapolated canton Zürich are lowland regions with comparatively flat topography, so the variation in slope and ruggedness across the study area is small and the available GPS data may simply not contain enough contrast in these variables for the model to detect an effect. This does not mean that terrain is irrelevant to wild boar movement in general. In a landscape with hard elevation differences, steep slopes and rugged terrain, these covariantes will likely have an influence on animal movement by increasing the energetic cost of travel and by channelling routes along valleys, ridgelines or passes. A model calibrated on telemetry from such a region would likely find these covariates both significant and informative. The result obtained here should therefore be read as a property of the specific training area rather than as evidence that terrain can be safely ignored when transferring or rebuilding the model for landscapes with more substantial relief.

The weak but significant avoidance of water in the model fits well to wild boar ecology. In in-patch movement wild boar are generally drawn to water bodies for drinking, wallowing and the cover offered by riparian vegetation. However, since the model captures only the in-matrix steps, where the animals move directly between patches rather than foraging or resting, a lake or a river can represent a minor obstacle that has to be circumvented or crossed at a suitable point. This adds a small energetic and time cost to an otherwise direct path, which is enough to produce a weak preference for routes that stay slightly farther from water during fast, directional movement, even though the same animals may seek out water intensively in other behavioral states.

The consistent avoidance of railways, settlements, main roads and secondary roads during in-matrix transit shows one shared underlying behaviour rather than four independent effects. All four covariates represent sources of human presence, noise and physical disruption, and wild boar are well documented to treat such features as zones of elevated risk that they cross only when necessary. The particularly strong effect for distance to railway likely reflects both the physical hazard of crossing active tracks and the fact that rail corridors often run through open, exposed terrain that offers little cover during a crossing attempt, which is precisely the kind of situation a transiting animal would want to minimise. Distance to settlement showed a comparable pattern, consistent with the broader finding that proximity to human settlements is one of the strongest predictors of reduced wild boar occupancy and habitat use. Assigning dense settlements the maximum resistance in the burn-in phase rather than NoData is a deliberate compromise: wild boar do enter urban fringes nocturnally (Guberti et al. 2019; Morelle et al. 2015), so treating settlement as an absolute barrier would suppress pathways that occur in reality, and pathways routed through such cells should be read as rare but possible. The weaker but still significant avoidance of main and secondary roads fits the same logic on a smaller scale. Roads bring traffic noise, lighting and the chance of direct encounters with humans or vehicles, and although they are far easier to cross physically than a railway or a built-up area, they still represent a brief moment of high exposure for an otherwise cover-seeking species. Together, these four effects suggest that wild boar in transit actively route themselves around the infrastructure network, trading a longer or more indirect path for reduced exposure to human-associated risk, which is exactly the kind of behavioral trade-off a resistance-based connectivity model is meant to capture.

The exclusion of distance to fenced roads in the iSSF is a transferable methodological point. Including it produced an ecologically implausible apparent attraction toward motorway fencing, an artifact of animals pacing along impassable barriers and generating dense telemetry clusters. This pacing behaviour likely arises because a fenced motorway represents a hard edge in an otherwise continuous landscape. When wild boar reach such a barrier while moving along a familiar route, they do not simply turn back. Instead, they tend to follow the fence line in search of a gap, an underpass or some other point where the obstacle can be crossed, which produces repeated, movement directly alongside the infrastructure. A model that only sees the resulting concentration of GPS fixes near the fence has no way of distinguishing this search behaviour from genuine habitat preference, and ends up interpreting the barrier itself as an attractive feature. Handling such hard barriers through the deterministic burn-in rather than as a step-selection covariate is the correct response, and any iSSF study in a landscape with dense linear barriers should apply the same check before finalising variable selection.

The two movement kernel terms, log_sl and cos_ta, were both highly significant in the final model. This indicates that step length and turning angle carry genuine information about how wild boar move during in-matrix transit, beyond what is explained by the landscape covariates alone. Their significance confirms that including these terms in the iSSF was necessary. Without them, part of the habitat selection signal could have simply reflected the animal’s typical movement pattern rather than an actual preference for certain landscape features. By accounting for the intrinsic step-length distribution and the degree of directional persistence directly in the model, the remaining covariate effects can be interpreted as genuine habitat selection during transit, separated from the mechanics of the movement process itself.

5.2 Resistance Surface Quality and Transfer Validity

The Aargau resistance surface reproduces the expected corridor structure, with low resistance along forested areas, intermediate resistance across the agricultural plain and high resistance in and around urban areas. The median resistance of 35.1 lies clearly above the scale midpoint of 27.8, which suggests that during transit wild boar experience most of the landscape as costly rather than neutral. This fits the broader idea of a landscape of fear, in which an animal’s route choice while moving between patches is shaped less by the search for good habitat and more by the need to minimise exposure to risk. Wild boar do not migrate by preference. Long-distance transit through open or human-influenced terrain exposes them to disturbance and potential mortality, so they undertake it only when a change of patch is necessary, and they try to keep the associated exposure as low as possible. This is also visible in the structure of the iSSF results themselves. In-patch behaviour reflects a search for the best available option within a landscape the animal already knows and uses regularly, whereas in-matrix behaviour is dominated by avoidance. Of the covariates retained in the final model, only the two forest density measures act as attractants, while distance to railway, settlement, main road, secondary road and water all represent features the animal moves away from. The asymmetry between two attracting and several avoiding covariates is consistent with a transit phase in which the priority is not to find an optimal location but to reach the next patch while passing through as little risk as possible.

Transferring the Aargau parameters to Zürich is operationally necessary because no Zürich telemetry was available, and it rests on the assumption that wild boar respond to forest and infrastructure similarly in both cantons. This is plausible given their shared Mittelland structure and the broadly consistent habitat preferences of European wild boar (Morelle et al. 2015), but the more intensively urbanised Greater Zürich landscape may host animals with modified avoidance responses that the Aargau coefficients do not capture. Reusing the Aargau scaling parameters for the z-standardisation is what keeps the beta coefficients interpretable across the transfer, since the covariates are then expressed on the same scale on which they were originally estimated. The final normalisation, however, is computed on the Zürich-specific eta range rather than the Aargau range. This choice means that the most suitable Zürich forest cells are mapped to R = 1 and the full [0, 1] suitability scale is used within the canton, which is exactly what a tool meant to identify corridors inside Zürich needs. The cost is that the resulting resistance values can no longer be compared directly to their Aargau counterparts, since a specific value, no longer represents the same relative habitat quality in both rasters. For a tool that is explicitly scoped to intra-cantonal spatial planning in Zürich, this loss of cross-cantonal comparability is an acceptable trade-off.

The Keeley constant of C = 4 was adopted from Fischer et al. (2024), where it was calibrated for red deer rather than wild boar. There is no published wild-boar-specific value for this exponent, and the way a species translates habitat suitability into perceived movement cost can plausibly differ between a large, wide-ranging ungulate such as red deer and a smaller, more generalist species such as wild boar. Ecologically, C controls how strongly small differences in habitat suitability are amplified into differences in movement cost. A higher value stretches the resistance scale into a steep curve, so that cells just below the suitability optimum are already treated as considerably more costly, which concentrates modelled movement into narrow corridors through the very best habitat. A lower value would flatten this relationship, letting the model treat moderately suitable areas as only slightly more costly than optimal ones and producing broader, more diffuse corridors. Because wild boar are a comparatively flexible, generalist species that is known to use a wider range of habitat types than red deer, it is conceivable that their real perception of cost rises less steeply with declining suitability, which would argue for a smaller C than the one used here. Using C = 4 is therefore a pragmatic choice rather than a species-calibrated one. It draws on a value that has already been applied successfully in a comparable connectivity modelling context and produces a resistance range that is wide enough to differentiate corridors meaningfully, but it should be read as a reasonable working assumption rather than as an empirically derived parameter for wild boar, and one that may overestimate how selective the species actually is when moving through the landscape.

5.3 The Planning Tool

The central advance over the static models of Fischer et al. (2024) and Urbina et al. (2023) is interactivity. Both of these studies provide rigorous maps of the current landscape, but neither can answer the what-if questions that ASF management actually needs answered. The plugin closes this gap. A user can draw a fence across a pinchpoint, re-run the model, and see directly how the corridor backbone, the infection-risk zones, and the contamination surface respond to that single change. This addresses the trade-off identified by Hess (1994), in which the same corridor that benefits a species can also accelerate disease, by showing both sides of a specific intervention rather than presenting one general map that has to stand in for every possible scenario.

The plugin brings together five analytical modules that complement rather than duplicate each other. Each one approaches the question of connectivity from a different angle, and each carries its own strengths and blind spots. The baseline run shown in Figure 5.2 illustrates this side by side, and the following paragraphs walk through what each module contributes and where its results need to be read with caution.

The least-cost path module traces a clear backbone radiating outward from the outbreak origin toward the surrounding habitat destinations, which gives an immediate overview of where the landscape is currently most permeable. As an overview tool this works well, but the single traced route should not be mistaken for a realistic depiction of how an actual animal moves. No wild boar has complete knowledge of the cost surface across an entire canton, and none travels the literal cheapest line from origin to destination. A further limitation is that the method only ever returns the one optimal route between a pair of points. If two or more paths through the landscape are nearly equally permeable, the module still highlights just one of them as the backbone, and the existence of a viable alternative corridor running alongside it stays invisible in the output.

Circuit theory contributes exactly the perspective that a single path cannot, since it reveals bottlenecks and redundancy across the whole corridor network rather than picking out one preferred route (McRae et al. 2008). The pinchpoint panel concentrates current density into a small number of bright branches that mark the structurally most important crossings. At the same time, the brightest of these branches in this run follows the valley floor, where the basemap shows the corridor passing close to settled ground rather than staying within forest cover. This is a direct illustration of how the method’s sensitivity to the way resistance values combine across parallel and series paths in the underlying resistor network can make a route through the edge of a built-up area register as the dominant pinchpoint, even though it is not a route a wild boar would ever take in practice. The pinchpoint layer therefore has to be read alongside the land cover rather than taken at face value. On top of this interpretive caveat, the method also depends on an external Circuitscape.jl process, so its availability and runtime are tied to whether a working Julia installation can be found on the user’s system, with the slower scipy solver stepping in as a fallback.

The infection-risk module expresses cost-distance as an EFSA-anchored probability that veterinary authorities can read directly, which is its main strength as a planning tool. This strength comes with a limit of its own. Because the EFSA zone radii are fixed regulatory distances rather than empirically derived thresholds, a continuous surface calibrated to reproduce those same radii mainly shows how the irregular landscape reshapes an idealised circular buffer, rather than supplying a genuinely new estimate of risk. The panel makes this point visually, since its three zone boundaries closely follow the terrain instead of forming the regular rings that a fixed-radius rule would draw on its own. A second point worth keeping in mind is that the conversion from cost to risk rescales the published dispersal distance using the median resistance of the analysis window. Because that median shifts whenever the user changes the outbreak origin or adds a landscape modification, the same nominal risk value can correspond to a different real-world distance from one scenario run to the next, which makes a direct numerical comparison between runs less straightforward than it first appears.

The biased correlated random walk offers an independent stochastic check that converges toward the circuit-theory result at the theoretical limit (Saerens et al. 2009). The corresponding panel shows a dense, well-defined core directly around the origin, which is reassuring on its own. It also shows a faint halo of thin trailing paths extending well beyond the forested ridge into the more open and settled terrain to the south and west. This is exactly the kind of drift away from the expected forest corridor that suggests the cost and persistence weights alone do not always keep the walk on ecologically plausible paths, with the stochastic component occasionally overriding the pull of the landscape. A related consequence is that the method can struggle to register small, localised mitigation features such as a single wildlife overpass. Because the walk has no knowledge of where such a feature lies and only reacts to the cost and direction immediately around each walker, a structure placed near the edge of the simulated spread may simply not be visited by enough of the released individuals to leave a visible trace in the density surface, even though it would in fact provide a usable crossing.

The individual-based model adds an epidemiological layer by simulating disease trajectories conditioned on an ASF infectious period, and its panel is the most telling of the five in one particular respect. Alongside the contiguous high-probability zone immediately around the origin, a second, clearly separated cluster of low-probability cells appears further to the southeast, with no continuous path of finite resistance connecting it to the main mass. Because the agents in this model select their next step purely by endpoint suitability, without checking whether the straight line to that endpoint crosses an impassable cell, an infected agent can occasionally appear to step across a barrier such as a motorway or a large lake that should be entirely impermeable. This detached cluster is best read as a visible instance of that artifact rather than as a real, independently reached focus of contamination. Choosing the published Circuitscape.jl solver, with settings matching those of Urbina et al. (2023), favours a validated implementation over a custom one, and the scipy fallback keeps the capability available where Julia cannot be installed. A further point to bear in mind is that, like the random walk, this is a stochastic simulation. A single run with a fixed number of agents represents one draw from a wider range of possible outcomes, and the exact shape of the contamination surface, including incidental artefacts like the detached cluster described above, can shift somewhat between runs even when the landscape and parameters stay the same.

5.4 Limitations and Sources of Uncertainty

The most significant limitation is the absence of formal validation. The iSSF fit was not quantified with concordance statistics or spatial cross-validation, and predictive performance on held-out Aargau steps was not assessed, so the relationship between modeled resistance and actual movement probability remains uncertain (Avgar et al. 2016). The Zürich surface is entirely unvalidated, since no Zürich tracks were available to test the transferred corridors, which adds a layer of uncertainty to management decisions based on the Zürich map.

A gap of roughly ten years separates the 2014 to 2017 telemetry from the 2026 landscape data, so infrastructure and land cover changes are not reflected in the behavioural calibration, and their net effect was not assessed. The 15-minute fix interval reliably captures directed transit (Podgórski et al. 2013; Benhamou 2014) but the conservative classifier may exclude some shorter in-matrix steps, slightly underestimating selection strength, though the large retained sample and high significance of all predictors suggest the coefficients are robust. Finally, the plugin modules carry their own assumptions: the infectious-period distribution is calibrated to Eastern European and Baltic populations (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) 2018; Probst et al. 2017; Chenais et al. 2019) whose disease course may differ from ASF-naive Swiss wild boar, and the static movement kernel does not capture nocturnal or seasonal variation in space use.

5.5 Practical Implications

The tool is primarily a pre-outbreak planning instrument. Its main value is enabling authorities to identify high-risk corridors and prioritise fence locations before an incursion, with the pinchpoint map showing where a small barrier achieves the greatest reduction in connectivity and the infection-risk raster showing which zones would require surveillance under EFSA guidelines. The scenario comparison supports the iterative decision-making that operational management needs, letting an officer test alternative fence alignments in a single session without programming or reprocessing.

The pipeline follows the FAIR principles of the ZHAW reproducibility requirement, drawing on publicly accessible federal geodata except for the partner-provided telemetry and fence data, with the full workflow encoded in documented notebooks and the plugin distributed as an installable QGIS package reusable in other cantons.

5.6 Outlook

Several extensions would add further value to the pipeline.

On the methodological side, the step classification could be revisited using Hidden Markov Models. This approach was explored during the project and then set aside, both because of its added complexity and because the rule-based classifier already produced convincing, interpretable results at much lower implementation cost. An HMM-based classification could still serve as an independent validation of the current rules or as a refinement in a future iteration of the pipeline.

The covariate set itself also leaves room for improvement. A true forest density layer derived directly from canopy data would likely outperform the proxy used here, and several infrastructure-related variables could be made more realistic, including the width and passability of rivers, traffic volume on roads as a proxy for disturbance intensity, and a distinction between small settlements that wild boar can and do cross and larger urban areas that act as effective barriers.

Because the cantonal boundary is an artificial cut in an otherwise continuous landscape, incorporating spatial data from the adjacent German border region would help reduce the edge effects that currently limit the validity of the resistance surface near the northern cantonal edge.

The model would also benefit from a stronger temporal dimension. A day and night distinction could capture the marked differences in wild boar activity and risk perception between these periods, both of which are likely to shape transit routes in ways the current model cannot represent. Agricultural and crop-related factors were deliberately left out of these considerations, since they primarily influence in-patch behaviour rather than the directed transit movement this model focuses on.

Finally, two extensions remain particularly important for moving from a methodologically sound prototype to an operationally validated tool. The highest priority is obtaining GPS telemetry from Zürich wild boar to validate the transferred surface against independent movement data. Beyond that, weighting the outputs by estimated wild boar density would add the host-abundance dimension that, together with connectivity, drives epidemic spread (Guberti et al. 2019).
